\section{Interaction \& Maneuver Extraction}

\subsection{Encounter Retrieval}
Encounter between traffic participants were identified as temporally overlapping pairs whose mutual distance falls below a predefined threshold \cite{perez_castro_empirical_2025, mohammed_characterization_2019}.

To enable repeated neighborhood queries, support SSM feature computation and ensure scalability across multiple entities, trajectories were indexed using a STR-Tree \cite{deng_trajectory_2011}. Prior to indexing, trajectories were geometrically simplified with the Douglas-Peucker algorithm, reducing computational load while preserving their characteristics \cite{zheng_trajectory_2015}.

For each target trajectory, candidate encounters were retrieved via spatial buffer queries and subsequently validated by computing timestamp-aligned relative distances and velocities. The resulting encounter set forms the basis for interaction and maneuver identification as well as surrogate safety analysis.


\subsection{Interactions}
Encounters were refined into interactions using established filtering criteria \cite{kutsch_analyzing_2025}. An interaction must satisfy:
\begin{enumerate}
	\item At least one timestamp with inter-object distance <20m and no timestamp exceeding 40m
	\item Minimum temporal overlap of 5 seconds
	\item Each object travels at least 10m
\end{enumerate} 

Periods of zero velocity were removed to exclude stationary states \cite{mohammadnazar_classifying_2021, li_volatility-based_2025}. Additionally, timestamps with velocities below average walking speed (1.4-1.48 m/s) \cite{bartels_bewegungsverhalten_2014} were excluded to avoid inaccurate maneuvers caused by queueing behavior or congestion effects.

\subsection{Maneuvers}
A maneuver $M$ is defined as a temporally bounded driving or riding event performed by an ego participant $e$. Formally, a maneuver is represented by the tuple
$$M = (e,[t_\text{start},t_\text{end}], \tau, \mathcal{C},\mathbf{f}_M)$$
where  $\tau$ denotes the maneuver type, $\mathcal{C}$ optional contextual entities and $\mathbf{f}_M$ the maneuver-specific descriptors.
Depending on the maneuver type, the context can include another traffic participant (e.g. following, overtaking) and/or environmental elements (e.g. turning at an intersection).

This abstraction seperates maneuver structure (temporal bounds and context) from maneuver semantics (type-specific descriptors), ensuring consistent handling across maneuver types and facilitating downstream processing.


Most studies characterize interaction-based maneuvers (following, overtaking) using longitudinal and lateral distances defined in a relative coordinate system from the perspective of the maneuver-executing participant \cite{khan_characteristics_2001, kutsch_analyzing_2025, mohammed_characterization_2019}. This representation reduces dependency on global orientation and isolates the relative motion between interacting cyclists.

For two interacting traffic participants $e$ (ego) and $o$ (other), let $\theta_e(t)$ be the heading angle of $e$. Then the longitudinal and lateral distances are computed as:
$$\Delta x(t) = x_o(t) - x_e(t), \quad \Delta y(t) = y_o(t) - y_e(t)$$
\begin{align*}
	d_{\text{long}}(t) &= \Delta x(t)\cos\theta_e(t) + \Delta y(t)\sin\theta_e(t) \\
	d_{\text{lat}}(t)  &= -\Delta x(t)\sin\theta_e(t) + \Delta y(t)\cos\theta_e(t)
\end{align*}

Smoothing the longitudinal and lateral signals with a Gaussian kernel ($\sigma$ = 0.6s) has been found to reduce zero-crossings caused by noise and improve the identification of maneuver start and end points.


\subsubsection{Following Interactions}

Following interactions describe periods in which an ego participant maintains a consistent leader-follower relationship with another participant. Formally, a following interaction $M_F$ is a maneuver $M$ with context $\mathcal{C}=\{o\}$, where $o$ is the leader.

Candidate intervals are identified by analyzing the longitudinal displacement in the ego-centric frame for sign flips, which indicate changes in relative ordering \cite{mohammed_characterization_2019}. Additional constraints ensure meaningful following behavior, including:

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Maximum Time Headway:} threshold seperating constrained from unconstrained riding, typically around 5-6 s \cite{hoogendoorn_bicycle_2016,kutsch_analyzing_2025, bachir_identifying_2025}
	\item \textbf{Spatial constraints:} $|d_{\text{lat}}(t)|\leq 1$m and 1m $\leq d_{\text{long}}(t) \leq$ 30m
	\item \textbf{Directional consistency:} relative heading difference $\leq$ 35° \cite{feng_automatic_2023, kutsch_analyzing_2025}
	\item \textbf{Persistence:} criteria must hold for 1s 
	
\end{itemize}

The approach ensures robust detection of following behaviors by combining spatial, temporal, and directional constraints.

\subsubsection{Overtaking Maneuver}

An overtaking maneuver describes a maneuver in which an ego participant $e$ moves from a trailing to a leading position relative to another participant $o$. Formally, an overtaking maneuver $M_O$ is a maneuver $M$ with context $\mathcal{C}=\{o, t_c\}$. The overtaking point $t_c$ denotes the timestamp at which the relative longitudinal ordering changes.

\paragraph{Detection Principle}
Overtaking is identified through the ego-centric longitudinal displacement $d_{\text{long}}(t)$. A candidate event is detected when a persistent sign change from positive to negative occurs, indicating a transition from trailing to leading position. To reduce false detections caused by noise or oscillations around zero, sign flips must persist over multiple consecutive frames. Each candidate window is then refined to determine maneuver start and end points. 

\paragraph{Automated Edge Detection}
Unlike the rule-based procedure proposed by \citeauthor{kutsch_analyzing_2025} (\citeyear{kutsch_analyzing_2025}), which was designed for precise analytical boundary identification and relied on fixed lateral distance and velocity thresholds combined with manual verification, the present work requires a fully automated and noise-robust detection strategy suitable for integration into a multi-stage clustering framework.

While the approach of \citeauthor{kutsch_analyzing_2025} enables highly accurate edge definitions for detailed scenario analysis, it is sensitive to manual threshold calibration and not directly scalable to large datasets or modular unsupervised pipelines. The objective here is therefore not maximal geometric precision but robust and reproducible maneuver segmentation.

Given a detected overtaking point $t_c$, maneuver start and end frames are determined via an adaptive threshold search on the absolute lateral displacement signal $|d_\text{lat}(t)|$.

The procedure follows three principles:

\begin{enumerate}
	\item \textbf{Local search window} A bounded backward-forward search (minimum and maximum frame limits) ensures temporal locality around $t_c$
	\item \textbf{Logarithmic adaptive thresholds} Instead of using fixed lateral distance cutoffs, a set of logarithmically spaced thresholds is generated between a minimal displacement (e.g. 0.05m) and the observed lateral distance at crossing point.
	This allows the algorithm to adapt to maneuver magnitude while remaining robust to noise and small oscillations.
	\item \textbf{Sanity validation checks} Candidate edges are accepted only if:
	\begin{itemize}
		\item longitudinal displacement evolves monotonically through the maneuver
		\item minimum lateral distance at overtaking point
		\item maximum overall lateral displacement
		\item duration exceeds a minimum threshold
	\end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}

Operationally, the algorithm searches backward from $t_c$ for the last frame, whose lateral displacement falls below the smallest feasible adaptive threshold and analogously forward for the end frame. If validation criteria are not satisfied, the candidate is discarded.

For reproducibility, the following parameters were used: a backward-forward search window of 10-125 frames, a minimum lateral separation at crossing of 0.8m, a maximum lateral displacement of 3m and a set of five logarithmically spaced lateral thresholds ranging from 0.05m to 1.2m.

The design prioritizes automation compatibility, scale robustness and reproducibility, making it suitable for hierarchical clustering applications.

For validated and complete overtaking maneuvers, the descriptive feature vector $\mathbf{f}_{M_O}$ is extracted and the full feature specification is provided in the Appendix.

\section{Hierarchical Riding Style Identification}


This section describes the hierarchical multi-stage pipeline, which is applied to both maneuvers individually.

\subsection{Window-Based Behavioral Representation}

The first stage of the hierarchical pipeline consists of segmenting each maneuver into short-term temporal windows and extracting descriptive features that characterize local riding behavior. This approach operationalizes the concept of micro-behavioral states, which capture short term variations in rider dynamics while preserving temporal continuity \cite{li_volatility-based_2025, bachir_identifying_2025, mohammadnazar_classifying_2021}.

\subsubsection{Window Segmentation}
Each maneuver is segmented using a sliding window of 3-second duration with 50\% overlap. 
This window length balances capturing short-term riding volatility and reflecting longer-term behavioral trends, following the findings of \citeauthor{li_volatility-based_2025} (\citeyear{li_volatility-based_2025}), who demonstrated that 1-second windows are suitable for detecting rapid fluctuations, 5-second windows for sustained aggressive patterns and 3-second windows achieve an optimal trade-off between the two. 
The resulting windows provide a consistent temporal resolution for extracting local behavioral states, which serve as the input for regime discovery and subsequent aggregation.

\subsubsection{Feature Sets}
\label{meth:window-feature-set}

Each window is described by three distinct feature sets: riding dynamics, traffic context and infrastructure characteristics. These sets are treated independently to preserve domain seperation, ensuring that subsequent clustering reflects meaningful behavioral variation rather than the environment.

Each behavioral state is characterized by internal and external (environment) factors that make up a local behavioral state. The environment is defined by traffic and infrastructure related features.
Each feature set is standalone and serves a different purpose.

\paragraph{Riding Dynamics}

Riding dynamics capture the internal state of the ego cyclist and are derived from kinematic signals such as speed, longitudinal and lateral acceleration and yaw rate \cite{arvin_how_2019, arvin_safety_2021, bachir_identifying_2025}. 
Volatility features quantify short-term variability and fluctuations within each window. 
For each signal, the sample standard deviation, mean absolute deviation, coefficient of variation and quartile coefficient of variation are computed (Table~\ref{tab:volatility}). 
These metrics provide a principled basis for clustering windows into local behavioral regimes, reflecting micro-behavioral variation rather than absolute magnitude differences. Additionally, magnitude metrics are applied to each kinematic signal, which quantify motion intensity and include minimum, maximum and mean.


\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Magnitude features} quantify motion intensity, including mean, minimum, maximum and percentile values for speed, acceleration and yaw rate. 
	\item \textbf{Volatility metrics} reflect the temporal irregularity of motion, which has been shown to correlate with riding style, maneuver execution and behavioral risk \cite{mohammadnazar_classifying_2021, li_volatility-based_2025, kamrani_extracting_2018, yarlagadda_exploring_2022}.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Traffic Context}
\label{meth:traffic}
The surrounding traffic environment is characterized using surrogate safety measures (SSMs), which quantify the operational risk posed by other traffic participants \cite{wang_review_2021}. For each timestamp within a window, proximity counts, minimal Time-To-Collision (TTC), minimal Distance-At-Closest-Approach (DCA) and Deceleration-To-Avoid-Crash (DRAC) are computed relative to all participants.
These measures are aggregated across the window to produce statistics such as mean, maximum, percentile thresholds and exposure fractions. SSMs are computed seperately for different participant classes (cyclists, pedestrians, motorized vehicles), enabling later analysis of cluster-specific interactions with distinct traffic types. 


\paragraph{Infrastructure Characteristics}
Infrastructure features describe the surrounding road environment, derived from the OpenDrive file associated with the TUMDOT-MUC dataset. Each window is characterized by metrics such as lane width, lane width entropy, lane type, lane type entropy \cite{bachir_identifying_2025}, relative lane position, offset from lane center, distance to the nearest traffic light and proportion of time spent on different surface types (bike lane, sidewalk, roadway). Empirical studies indicate that infrastructure characteristics substantially influence rider behavior and maneuver execution \cite{belikhov_exploring_2025, wang_investigating_2022, gossling_subjectively_2022}.

\subsubsection{Scenario Separation}
Riding dynamics vary considerably between intersections and straight road segments due to differences in environmental complexity, traffic density and rider decision-making. Empirical studies support this distinction: \citeauthor{li_volatility-based_2025} (\citeyear{li_volatility-based_2025}) observed that entropy of driving style distributions is consistently higher in intersection scenarios, indicating greater diversity and uncertainty in behavior compared to non-intersections. Similarly, \citeauthor{mohammadnazar_classifying_2021} (\citeyear{mohammadnazar_classifying_2021}) found that driving styles differ across roadway contexts, with environmental factors such as land use, street type and surrounding traffic contributing to variations in driver behavior and volatility.  

Motivated by these findings, windows are categorized into \textbf{intersection} and \textbf{non-intersection} groups based on the distance to the nearest traffic light, which provides a reliable proxy for intersection proximity. Windows in each category are clustered separately to capture context-specific behavioral regimes. Subsequent maneuver-level aggregation, however, considers all windows jointly, ensuring that the final maneuver representation reflects the full spectrum of local behaviors while preserving the influence of scenario-specific complexity.


\subsubsection{Window Representation}
Let a maneuver $m$ be segmented into $T_m$ overlapping windows indexed by $t = 1, \ldots,T_m$.
Each window is defined as the tuple
$$w_t = \left(t_\text{start}^{(t)}, t_\text{end}^{(t)}, s_t, \mathbf{x}_t\right)$$
where $t_\text{start}^{(t)}$ and $t_\text{end}^{(t)}$ denote the temporal bounds of the window, $s_t\in S = \{I,N\}$ the scenario identifier (intersection or non-intersection) and 
$$\mathbf{x}_t = \left(x_t^\text{riding}, x_t^\text{traffic}, x_t^\text{infrastructure}\right)$$
the concatenated feature representation derived from riding, dynamics, traffic context and infrastructure characteristics.
The set of all windows belonging to maneuver $m$ is denoted as
$$\mathcal{W}_m=\{w_1, \ldots,w_{T_m}\}.$$
This representation forms the input space for local regime discovery described in the subsequent section.


\subsection{General Clustering Pipeline}
\label{meth:clustering-pipeline}
This section describes the generalized unsupervised clustering pipeline used in both clustering stages. The procedure is domain-independent and modular, allowing individual components to be replaced without affecting the overall structure. The pipeline is intentionally restricted to standard, well-established methods to ensure interpretability, reproducibility and computational robustness.

\paragraph{Feature Filtering}

To control multicollinearity and reduce redundancy, an iterative backward filtering procedure is applied.

First, features with high pairwise absolute correlation are removed. For each correlated pair exceeding a predefined threshold, the feature with the larger average correlation to all remaining features is discarded \cite{guyon_introduction_2003}.

Second, multicollinearity is further mitigated using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF). Among features exceeding the VIF threshold, the feature withe the largest value is removed \cite{cheng_variable_2022}. 

Both steps are repeated until all features satisfy the specified thresholds. The resulting feature matrix is standardized prior to dimensionality reduction.
\paragraph{Dimensionality Reduction}

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) is applied to obtain an orthogonal low-dimensional representation of the filtered feature space.

Dimensionality is determined via an explained variance threshold. Whitening is applied to normalize component variance, ensuring isotropic scaling in the transformed space.

Clustering is performed in PCA space.

\paragraph{Clustering}

Partition-based clustering is performed using K-means.

K-means was selected because:
\begin{itemize}
	\item It provides stable centroid-based partitions
	\item It scales efficiently to large datasets
	\item It yields directly interpretable cluster prototypes
	\item Preliminary experiments showed no consistent advantage of density-b
\end{itemize}
Clustering is repeated with multiple random initializations to mitigate local minima effects.

While Gaussian Mixture Models and DBSCAN were considered, preliminary experiments indicated that K-means achieved comparable or better separation of micro-behavioral regimes on the current trajectory features.

\paragraph{Clustering Stability}
Since no ground truth labels are available, robustness under perturbation is used as the primary reliability criterion.

Clustering stability is quantified using the Adjusted Rand Index (ARI) under three perturbation mechanism:
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Subsample stability:} Clustering is recomputed on repeated 80\% subsamples. ARI is computed relative to the reference clustering restricted to the same subset
	\item \textbf{Initialization stability:} Independent runs with different random seeds are compared
	\item \textbf{Noise stability:} Additive Gaussian noise scaled by feature-wise standard deviation is applied before reclustering.
\end{itemize}

Each stability measure is repeated across multiple runs. Median, mean, standard deviation and quantiles of ARI are reported.

Stability is interpreted as robustness of the partition under data and algorithmic perturbation rather than as a performance metric.

\subsection{Stage 1: Behavioral State Discovery}
\label{meth:level1}

Building upon established sliding-window segmentation approaches (Section \ref{rel:regimes}), the first abstraction layer formalizes local regime discovery as a clustering problem.

Let $\mathcal{W}_m = {w_1, \dots, w_{T_m}}$ denote the set of windows for maneuver $m$. Each window is associated with a scenario label $s_t \in S = {I, N}$ and feature representation $\mathbf{x}_t$.

Since behavioral variability differs substantially between scenarios, clustering is performed separately for each scenario:
$$\mathcal{W}^{(s)} = \left\{w_t\in\mathcal{W}_m\mid s_t=s\right\}$$
For each scenario $s$, the general clustering pipeline (Section \ref{meth:clustering-pipeline}) is applied to the riding-dynamics feature subset $\mathbf{x}_t^\text{riding}$.

The clustering procedure yields $k_s$ scenario-specific regimes. Each window receives a regime assignment
$$r_t^{(s)} \in\left\{1,\ldots,k_s\right\}$$.
This produces a discrete regime sequence for $t\in T_m$
$$\tilde{r}_t = \left(s_t,r_t^{(s_t)}\right)$$,
which forms a globally consistent segmentation of riding behavior.

The number of regimes is not fixed a priori but determined through stability-based model selection. While prior studies commonly report 2-3 regimes \cite{}, the framework remains fully unsupervised and permits any $k_s$ supported by structural stability and interpretability.

While prior work has focused on regime identification or probabilistic transition modeling, the present work introduces a second abstraction step that transforms regime sequences into maneuver-level representations.

\subsection{Regime Aggregation and Maneuver-Level Representation}
\label{meth:aggregation}

While prior work demonstrates that sequences of discrete behavioral regimes encode meaningful structural information, aggregation strategies are typically limited to summary statistics computed within predefined windows or embedded in probabilistic state-transition models. 
Similar local regime aggregation strategies have been applied by \citeauthor{bachir_identifying_2025} (\citeyear{bachir_identifying_2025}), who summarized riding behavior clusters using cluster proportion and transition frequency entropy measures to characterize the sequencial character of local regimes.
Unlike Hidden Markov or Markov Chain models \cite{pentland_modeling_1999} that assume data are generated by an underlying stochastic process, the present method directly summarizes observed regime sequences via proportions, run lengths and entropy measures \cite{vegetabile_estimating_2019}, capturing behavioral heterogeneity without imposing generative assumptions.

\paragraph{Proportions and Exposure.}

The number of windows of maneuver $m$ occurring in scenario $s$ is defined as
\[
T_m^{(s)}
=
\sum_{t\in T_m} \mathbf{1}(s_t = s).
\]

Scenario exposure is defined as
\[
P_m(s)
=
\frac{T_m^{(s)}}{T_m}.
\]
This quantity captures the proportion of a maneuver occurring in each scenario.

The global regime proportion for regime $r \in \mathcal{R}$ is defined as
\[
P_m(r)
=
\frac{1}{T_m}
\sum_{t\in T_m}
\mathbf{1}(\tilde r_t = r).
\]

The set of regimes observed in maneuver $m$ is
\[
\mathcal{R}_m
=
\{ r \in \mathcal{R} \mid P_m(r) > 0 \}.
\]

For scenario-conditional analysis, the regime proportion within scenario $s$ is defined as
\[
P_m(r \mid s)
=
\frac{1}{T_m^{(s)}}
\sum_{t\in T_m}
\mathbf{1}(s_t = s \wedge \tilde r_t = r).
\]

The set of regimes observed within scenario $s$ in maneuver $m$ is
\[
\mathcal{R}_m^{(s)}
=
\{ r \in \mathcal{R} \mid P_m(r \mid s) > 0 \}.
\]

\paragraph{Temporal Persistence (Run Statistics).}

A run is defined as a maximal contiguous sequence of windows assigned to the same scenario-qualified regime $\tilde r_t$ within maneuver $m$.

Let $L_{r,i}$ denote the length of the $i$-th run of regime $r$ within maneuver $m$.  
For each regime $r \in \mathcal{R}_m$, the mean and standard deviation of run lengths are computed as
\[
\mu_r = \mathrm{mean}(L_{r,i}),
\qquad
\sigma_r = \mathrm{std}(L_{r,i}).
\]

The mean run length captures behavioral persistence, while the standard deviation reflects fragmentation variability.

\paragraph{Temporal Center of Mass.} 

To capture the temporal distribution of a regime within a maneuver, the normalized center of mass (COM) is computed. For a regime $r\in \mathcal{R}$ within scenario $s$, the normalized scenario-specific COM is defined as
\[
\text{COM}_r^{(s)} = 
\frac{1}{T_m^{(s,r)}} 
\sum_{t \in T_m \,:\, \tilde r_t = r \wedge s_t = s} \frac{t}{T_m^{(s)}},
\]
where $T_m^{(s,r)}$ is the number of windows in maneuver $m$ assigned to regime $r$ within scenario $s$, and $T_m^{(s)}$ is the total number of windows in $m$ occurring in scenario $s$. This measures the average temporal location of the regime within that scenario.

For a regime $r$ across the entire maneuver, ignoring scenario splits, the normalized center of mass is
\[
\text{COM}_r^{(m)} = 
\frac{1}{T_m^{(r)}} 
\sum_{t \in T_m \,:\, \tilde r_t = r} \frac{t}{T_m},
\]
where $T_m^{(r)}$ is the total number of windows in $m$ assigned to regime $r$, and $T_m$ is the total number of windows in maneuver $m$. This captures the true temporal distribution of the regime throughout the maneuver.

\paragraph{Entropy Measures.}
Following \citeauthor{bachir_identifying_2025}, Shannon entropy is used to quantify behavioral variability and unpredictability across regime distributions.
Global behavioral heterogeneity is quantified using normalized Shannon entropy:
$$H_\text{global}(m) = \frac{1}{\log_2|\mathcal{R}_m|} \cdot - \sum_{r\in\mathcal{R}_m} P_m(r) \log_2 P_m(r)$$

For $|\mathcal{R}_m| = 1$, entropy is defined as $0$.

Scenario-specific entropy is defined analogously as
$$H_s(m) = \frac{1}{\log_2|\mathcal{R}_m^{(s)}|} \cdot - \sum_{r\in\mathcal{R}_m^{(s)}} P_m\left(r|s\right) \log_2 P_m\left(r|s\right)$$

For $|\mathcal{R}_m^{(s)}| = 1$, entropy is defined as $0$.

\paragraph{Maneuver-Level Feature Vector.}

The maneuver-level regime aggregation representation is defined as
\[
\mathbf{z}_m
=
\Big(
H_{\text{global}}(m),
\left\{
	H_s(m),
	P_m(s)
\right\}_{s \in S},
\left\{
	P_m(r),
	\mu_r,
	\sigma_r,
	\text{COM}_r^{(m)},
	\text{COM}_r^{(s)}
\right\}_{r \in\mathcal{R}}
\Big).
\]

This representation jointly captures scenario exposure, regime composition, temporal persistence and behavioral heterogeneity, forming the partial input for maneuver-level clustering.

\subsection{Stage 2: Maneuver-Level Representation and Style Clustering}
While Level 1 identifies local behavioral regimes, maneuvers themselves represent structured sequences of such regimes. The second abstraction layer therefore transforms regime sequences into maneuver-level representations.

Rather than modeling transitions through generative assumptions (e.g., Hidden Markov Models \cite{pentland_modeling_1999}), the present approach summarizes observed regime sequences using descriptive statistics. This preserves interpretability and avoids assumptions about latent stochastic processes.

Given the regime sequence $\tilde{r}_{t=1}^{T_m}$ for maneuver $m$, the aggregation procedure (Section \ref{meth:aggregation}) computes:
\begin{itemize}
	\item Scenario exposure $P_m(s)$
	\item Regime proportions $P_m(r)$
	\item Run-length statistics $(\mu_r, \sigma_r)$
	\item Temporal centers of mass $\text{COM}_r$
	\item Entropy measures $H_{\text{global}}(m)$ and $H_s(m)$
\end{itemize}

These quantities collectively define the regime-structure vector:
$$\mathbf{z}_m^\text{regime}$$

To incorporate maneuver-type-specific characteristics (e.g. geometric or interaction features), the regime representation is concatenated with the maneuver-specific descriptors vector $\mathbf{f}_M$:
$$\mathbf{z}_m = \left(\mathbf{z}_m^\text{regime}, \mathbf{f}_M\right)$$

All maneuvers of the same type are then clustered using the general clustering pipeline.

This second clustering stage identifies maneuver-level styles, representing distinct execution modes characterized by internal behavioral structure rather than isolated kinematic measurements.

\subsection{Stage 3: Infrastructure-Conditioned Analysis}
The third stage evaluates how discovered regimes and maneuver-level styles relate to environment conditions. Clustering in Stages 1 and 2 is performed exclusively on behavioral features. Traffic and infrastructure variables are withheld from regime discovery to prevent environmental bias in behavioral abstraction.

Two complementary analysis are carried out:

\paragraph{(1) Window-Level Analysis}
Given fixed regime assignments $\tilde r_t$, traffic and infrastructure features are analyzed conditionally:
$$\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}_t^{\text{traffic}} \mid \tilde r_t = r], 
\quad 
\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}_t^{\text{infra}} \mid \tilde r_t = r]
$$
This reveals how local behavioral regimes manifest under different contextual conditions.

Because scenario labels are embedded in $\tilde r_t$, regime-context relations can be analyzed within and across scenarios.

\paragraph{(2) Maneuver-Level Analysis}
At the maneuver level, cluster-specific distributions of traffic and infrastructure descriptors are compared:
$$
\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}_m^{\text{traffic}} \mid c_m = k]
\quad
\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}_m^{\text{infra}} \mid c_m = k]
$$
where $c_m$ denotes the maneuver cluster label.

Analysis is exploratory and descriptive. Differences in means and dispersion are examined to identify systematic associations between maneuver styles and contextual exposure. 

No causal claims are made. The objective is characterization rather than inference.
