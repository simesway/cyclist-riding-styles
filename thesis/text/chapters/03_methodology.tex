\section{Interaction Retrieval}
The Analysis includes the extraction of special maneuvers (following, overtaking), but also the calculation of safety measures between cyclists and other traffic participants (cyclists, cars, pedestrians). Therefore encounters between traffic participants have to be extracted from the trajectory dataset. An encounter is a pair of traffic participants that coexist in a single timestamp \cite{perez_castro_empirical_2025} within a certain distance \cite{mohammed_characterization_2019}.

To efficiently index and search for potential trajectory encounters, a Spatio-Temporal R-Tree (STR-Tree) was used \cite{deng_trajectory_2011}, which serves as an optimized spatial indexing structure for querying spatial relationships between trajectories. 

Trajectories were initially simplified using the Douglas-Peucker algorithm, which reduces the number of trajectory points while maintaining the characteristic shape of the movement \cite{zheng_trajectory_2015}. The simplified trajectories are then split into segments at the remaining critical points, effectively reducing computational overhead while preserving the fundamental geometric properties of the original trajectories.

All the resulting segments were then used to construct an STR-Tree, which enables efficient spatial intersection queries. Given a target trajectory, a buffer zone is defined around it with a user-specific distance. The STR-Tree is queried for all segments intersecting this buffer, yielding candidate objects that may interact with the target.
To manage computational resources, the analysis was constrained to small batches of target trajectories, with the time window limited to maximum time window of the target trajectories.

For each candidate, relative distances and speeds \cite{perez_castro_empirical_2025} are computed at matching timestamps. These encounter metrics enable further domain specific filtering on these interaction candidates. The structured output can be used for further analysis, such as interaction and maneuver identification and the computation of surrogate safety metrics.

\section{Maneuvers}

All interaction candidates were additionally filtered by the three selection criteria from Kutsch et al. \cite{kutsch_analyzing_2025} to obtain interactions:
\begin{enumerate}
	\item Distance Constraint: Identified encounters with at least one timestamp where the absolute distance between center points was less than 20 m, while ensuring all timestamps maintained a maximum distance of 40 m.
	\item Temporal Criterion: Minimum 5-second trajectory overlap.
	\item Movement Validation: Each trajectory moves at least 10m.
	\item Heading Criterion: was used inside maneuver detection code
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Following}

The smoothed longitudinal displacement signal is analyzed for sign flips, which indicate transitions in relative positioning \cite{hoogendoorn_bicycle_2016, mohammed_characterization_2019}. The trajectory is divided into contiguous intervals between these zero crossings to focus on periods of consistent follower-leader ordering.

Additional spatial constraints were applied to restrict candidate interactions: the absolute lateral distance was required to remain below 1 m and the longitudinal distance to lie between 1 m and 30 m. Temporal filtering included a maximum Time Headway (THW) of 6 s, consistent with the headway-based separation of constrained and unconstrained riding reported in \cite{kutsch_analyzing_2025, bachir_identifying_2025}, and a minimum interaction duration of 1 s. Furthermore, a directional constraint was imposed, requiring the relative heading difference to remain below 35° \cite{feng_automatic_2023, kutsch_analyzing_2025}.


The candidates must fulfill all criteria for 13 consecutive timestamps to be labeled as Following Maneuvers. The interval detection method allows for multiple potential following segments within a single encounter pair, filtering out moments of non-moving. 
The candidates fulfilling all criteria for 13 consecutive timestamps (1 second) are labeled as Following Maneuvers.

The approach ensures robust detection of following behaviors by combining spatial, temporal, and directional constraints.

\subsection{Overtaking}

Longitudinal signals are analyzed to detect sign changes from positive to negative, indicating potential overtaking events \cite{kutsch_analyzing_2025, mohammed_characterization_2019}. To improve robustness, spurious crossings were filtered by requiring sign persistence over multiple frames, and temporally adjacent crossings were merged to account for oscillations around zero. 
Each detected crossing defined a window of interest centered on the longitudinal sign flip. These windows were refined by selecting start and end candidates based on local extrema of the longitudinal displacement and by limiting the temporal extent to ensure consistency.

\citeauthor*{kutsch_analyzing_2025} (\citeyear{kutsch_analyzing_2025}) proposed a detailed rule-based maneuver edge detection relying on fixed lateral distance and lateral velocity thresholds combined with manual verification. While effective for descriptive scenario analysis, such a procedure is difficult to integrate into a fully automated multi-stage clustering pipeline due to its sensitivity to threshold tuning and the reliance on manual inspection.

Therefore, a simplified and noise-robust edge detection scheme was developed. The method prioritizes robustness and automation compatibility over exact boundary precision. 
Differences in overtaking counts resulting from the modified edge definition reflect methodological choices rather than inconsistencies.


The maneuver boundaries are defined geometrically as the last frame in which the overtaking cyclist remains laterally aligned behind the leading cyclist, with the start and end corresponding to the frames where the lateral displacement approaches zero relative to the crossing configuration. Operationally, this is determined using the absolute lateral displacement signal with a logarithmically scaled set of adaptive thresholds.

Thresholds are generated dynamically based on the lateral distance at the overtaking point, ranging from 0.05 m to the observed maximum displacement (up to 1.5 m), with 5–7 logarithmically spaced levels providing stable performance. Edge detection proceeds backward and forward from the crossing point within constrained search ranges (minimum 10 frames, maximum 125–130 frames). Candidate maneuvers are validated through monotonicity checks of longitudinal displacement, lateral distance constraints (minimum 0.8 m at crossing \cite{perez_castro_empirical_2025}, maximum 3 m), minimum duration requirements, and trajectory consistency checks.

For validated candidates, the module extracts descriptive features \cite{khan_characteristics_2001}, with full details provided in \textbf{Appendix \ref{sec:overtaking_features}}. These features capture the nuanced dynamics of overtaking maneuvers across temporal, spatial and kinematic domains.

First the maneuvers were segmented using the sliding window approach, resulting in fixed-length temporal windows of 1s duration with 50\% overlap \cite{li_volatility-based_2025, mohammadnazar_classifying_2021}, a common approach for modeling short-term driving and riding dynamics while preserving temporal continuity. For each window three sets of features are extracted and aggregated, each used by a different stage of the analysis and separated cleanly to ensure domain separation. \subsection{Feature Selection}

To reduce multicollinearity and simplify the feature space, an iterative backward feature selection procedure was employed. In each iteration, pairs of features with high absolute correlation are identified, and one feature from each pair is removed \cite{guyon_introduction_2003}. The feature with higher mean correlation to all other features is discarded. Following correlation-based removal, multicollinearity is further mitigated by computing the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) on standardized features and removing the feature with the highest VIF exceeding the specified threshold \cite{cheng_variable_2022}. This two-step filtering is repeated until no features violate the thresholds \cite{dormann_collinearity_2013}.
The resulting feature set is then standardized passed to the next step in the pipeline.
\subsection{Principal Component Analysis}

PCA is applied before k‑means to reduce dimensionality and decorrelate features \cite{ding_k-means_2004}, with whitening to scale components to unit variance, improving clustering with distance-based algorithms \cite{montavon_learning_2012}. Ding \& He (2004) \cite{ding_k-means_2004} further show that k‑means centroids lie in the PCA subspace, providing a theoretical justification for this preprocessing.

\subsection{Clustering}

While Gaussian Mixture Models and DBSCAN were considered, preliminary experiments indicated that K-means achieved comparable or better separation of micro-behavioral regimes on the current trajectory features, with lower computational cost and fewer hyperparameters. As such, K-means was selected as the primary clustering algorithm for all stages, providing a robust and interpretable baseline for multi-stage aggregation.


\subsection{Clustering Stability}
Following the notion of clustering stability as robustness under data and algorithmic perturbations (von Luxburg, 2010) \cite{luxburg_clustering_2010}, clustering stability was assessed using the Adjusted Rand Index (ARI) under subsampling, random initialization, and additive noise.

For subsampling stability, a random subset comprising 80\% of the data was drawn without replacement. The clustering algorithm was refit on this subset, and ARI was computed between the resulting labels and the reference labels restricted to the same subset. To detect degenerate solutions, the minimum cluster fraction within each subsample was additionally recorded.

For seed stability, two clusterings were fit on the full dataset using different random initializations, and ARI was computed directly between their label assignments.

For noise stability, additive Gaussian noise was applied to the full dataset, with noise scaled per feature by its empirical standard deviation. The clustering algorithm was refit on the perturbed data, and ARI was computed relative to the reference clustering.

Each stability measure was repeated across multiple runs with independent random seeds. Stability was summarized using the median, mean, standard deviation, and the 10th and 90th percentiles of the ARI distributions. Cases resulting in a single cluster were treated as undefined.

Clustering quality was assessed separately using the silhouette score, computed once on a random subsample of the data using the reference clustering.


\subsection{Grid Search}
A grid search was conducted over feature-selection thresholds (correlation and variance inflation factor), PCA dimensionality, and the number of clusters to identify configurations that yield both interpretable and stable cluster structures.

For each hyperparameter combination, features were selected, dimensionality was reduced using PCA, and KMeans clustering was applied. Clustering stability was assessed over multiple runs using repeated subsampling and perturbations. Specifically, clustering was repeated n times on an 80\% stratified subsample of the data, and stability was quantified using the Adjusted Rand Index (ARI) under three conditions: variation due to subsampling, random noise, and random initialization.

Clustering quality was evaluated using the silhouette score computed on a subsample to reduce bias, while cluster balance was enforced by tracking the minimum relative cluster size across runs. For each metric, median values and variability were reported to obtain robust estimates and reduce sensitivity to outliers. This procedure allows selecting hyperparameters that balance clustering quality, stability, and cluster size constraints while mitigating overfitting. Using medians over repeated runs reduces the influence of stochastic variation inherent to KMeans and subsampling. 

Grid search was performed over feature-selection thresholds (correlation: 0.7–0.95, VIF: 3–100), PCA dimensionality (explained variance: 60–95\%), and the number of clusters k (2–5) to identify configurations that yield both interpretable and stable cluster structures.

The final clustering configuration was selected manually by inspecting cluster means and choosing the setup that provided the most interpretable groupings.

\subsection*{Level 1: Local Riding Behaviour (Window Level)}
\label{meth:level1}

Within each window, magnitude- \cite{enev_automobile_2016} and volatility-based features are extracted from kinematic signals.

Magnitude features describe the intensity of motion and include speed, longitudinal acceleration, and yaw rate. For each of these signals, minimum and maximum values within the window are computed.

Volatility features capture short-term variability and dynamic fluctuations. They are derived from speed, longitudinal and lateral acceleration, and rotational fluctuations. For each signal, the mean, mean absolute deviation, coefficient of variation, and quartile coefficient of variation are calculated.

All features are standardized, and magnitude and volatility features are weighted equally. Feature relevance is assessed using feature selection techniques and principal component analysis. The resulting window-level feature vectors are clustered separately per maneuver type. 

Yarlagadda and Pawar \cite{yarlagadda_exploring_2022} performed a two level clustering analysis seperating magnitude and volatility features.  In this paper magnitude and volatility features are clustered together, which allows the model to learn characteristic couplings between riding intensity and variability, yielding a set of local riding regimes that are globally consistent.

Each sliding window is then assigned a local riding regime corresponding to its cluster, producing a globally consistent segmentation of riding behavior. Cluster centers can be interpreted in the original feature units, facilitating analysis of characteristic riding patterns. Feature indices for magnitude and volatility are automatically managed to support both split and joint analyses.

\subsection*{Level 2: Maneuver-Level Representation}

\subsubsection*{Regime Dynamics Characterization}
To characterize the internal structure of each maneuver, an aggregation method was developed to transform the sequence of local regime assignments into quantitative descriptors of operational dynamics.
(Distribution-based behavioral summarization.)
By analyzing the temporal sequence of local regimes, features were extracted that capture the maneuver's behavioral complexity. The method quantifies regime dynamics through metrics that reveal the stability, transitions, and variability of the maneuver's internal operational states.
The internal structure of each maneuver was characterized by aggregating the sequence of local regime assignments into quantitative descriptors of operational dynamics. Features capturing behavioral complexity—such as volatility, transition frequency, and regime continuity—were extracted from the temporal sequence of regimes. Although these features resemble descriptors commonly used to analyze Markov chains, no probabilistic model was fitted; rather, the metrics summarize temporal dynamics and regime evolution for subsequent clustering.
Key computed features include:
\begin{compactitem}
	\item Proportion of windows with regime variations
	\item Frequency of regime transitions
	\item Characteristics of regime stability and continuity
\end{compactitem}
Specifically, the method generates metrics such as:
\begin{compactitem}
	\item Volatility proportion (percentage of non-stable regime windows)
	\item Transition rates between regime states
	\item Statistical properties of regime continuity, including mean and variance of volatile periods
\end{compactitem}

These features provide a nuanced representation of the maneuver's operational complexity, moving beyond simple state classification to capture the temporal evolution of interaction dynamics. The approach enables quantitative comparison of maneuvers based on their regime transition characteristics.

The resulting aggregation provides insights into the adaptability and consistency of vehicle interactions during overtaking scenarios, supporting further analysis of behavioral patterns.




The regime dynamics characterization enables a principled differentiation between single-regime and multi-regime maneuvers. Single-regime maneuvers are defined as those composed of only one riding regime throughout their duration. Importantly, this does not imply kinematic stability: a maneuver may remain entirely within a volatile regime and therefore exhibit highly dynamic behavior, yet still be classified as single-regime because no regime transition occurs. The distinction thus refers strictly to regime composition, not to overall motion smoothness or intensity.

In contrast, multi-regime maneuvers comprise at least two distinct regimes and therefore exhibit explicit transitions between internal behavioral states. This distinction follows the state-based perspective on human behavior proposed by Pentland and Liu \cite{pentland_modeling_1999}, where complex actions are understood as temporal compositions of transitions between a finite set of states. From this viewpoint, an overtaking maneuver emerges from the structured sequence of local riding regimes and their transitions rather than from raw kinematic signals alone.

Because single-regime maneuvers contain no regime transitions, they provide limited information about transition structure and adaptation dynamics. Consequently, clustering aimed at differentiating modes of maneuver behavior is performed exclusively on multi-regime maneuvers, where variations in transition frequency, volatility proportion, and regime continuity meaningfully reflect differences in interaction strategies.

To obtain a comprehensive maneuver-level representation, the regime-based descriptors are complemented by maneuver-specific features derived during the maneuver extraction process. This combined representation links the internal regime structure of a maneuver with its observable temporal, spatial, and kinematic characteristics.

Together, these elements form a compact yet expressive representation of maneuver behavior, capturing both the internal temporal structure of regime evolution and the overall execution characteristics of the maneuver.
