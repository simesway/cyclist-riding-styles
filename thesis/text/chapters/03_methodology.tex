\chapter{Methodology}



\section{Dataset}

This work is based on the TUMDOT–MUC dataset, which provides high-resolution trajectory data of multimodal urban traffic in Munich, collected using aerial drone observations. Multiple camera-equipped drones recorded traffic along an approximately 700~m urban corridor during afternoon peak hours on two weekdays. The recordings were then spatially and temporally merged to obtain continuous trajectories across the full observation area.

The dataset includes trajectories of diverse traffic participants, such as cars, buses, trucks, trams, motorcycles, pedestrians, cyclists, and e-scooters. Vulnerable road users (VRUs) make up a substantial portion, approximately 56~\% of all trajectories, making the dataset particularly suitable for analyzing cyclist behavior and interactions with other road users. Specifically, it contains 9,666 car trajectories, 8,792 pedestrian trajectories, and 4,747 bicycle trajectories.

For each detected road user, the dataset provides high-resolution trajectory information generated by computer vision–based detection and tracking algorithms. Each trajectory consists of frame-based observations, including a unique object identifier, object category, and spatial coordinates representing the road user’s position. Kinematic attributes such as velocity, acceleration, orientation, and object dimensions are also recorded, with a temporal resolution of 12.5~Hz.

In addition to trajectory data, the dataset includes a digital representation of the road infrastructure in OpenDRIVE (.xodr) format. This file provides a detailed description of the road network geometry and infrastructure elements within the observation area, including lane geometries, intersection structures, lane types and widths, traffic light locations, and road boundaries.

The observed study area represents a dense urban traffic environment with multimodal traffic and multiple intersections. The corridor features various infrastructure elements, including bicycle facilities, pedestrian crossings, tram tracks, and signalized intersections. Outside intersections, the bike lane runs adjacent to the sidewalk and is physically separated from the motorway by a parking lane.

Overall, the combination of high-resolution trajectories, diverse traffic participants, and detailed infrastructure data makes the TUMDOT–MUC dataset highly suitable for investigating microscopic interactions and maneuvering behavior of cyclists.

\section{Interaction \& Maneuver Extraction}

\subsection{Encounter Retrieval}
Encounter between traffic participants were identified as temporally overlapping pairs whose mutual distance falls below a predefined threshold \cite{perez_castro_empirical_2025, mohammed_characterization_2019}.

To enable repeated neighborhood queries, support SSM feature computation, and ensure scalability across multiple entities, trajectories were indexed using a STR-Tree, a spatial index that efficiently organizes geometric objects for fast querying \cite{deng_trajectory_2011}. Prior to indexing, trajectories were geometrically simplified with the Douglas-Peucker algorithm, which reduces the number of points while retaining the overall shape \cite{zheng_trajectory_2015}.

For each target trajectory, candidate encounters were retrieved via spatial buffer queries and subsequently validated by computing timestamp-aligned relative distances and velocities. The resulting encounter set forms the basis for interaction and maneuver identification as well as surrogate safety analysis.


\subsection{Interactions}
Encounters were refined into interactions using established filtering criteria \cite{kutsch_analyzing_2025}. An interaction must satisfy:
\begin{enumerate}
	\item At least one timestamp with inter-object distance <20m and no timestamp exceeding 40m
	\item Minimum temporal overlap of 5 seconds
	\item Each object travels at least 10m
\end{enumerate} 

Periods of zero velocity were removed to exclude stationary states \cite{mohammadnazar_classifying_2021, li_volatility-based_2025}. Additionally, timestamps with velocities below average walking speed (1.4-1.48 m/s) \cite{bartels_bewegungsverhalten_2014} were excluded to avoid inaccurate maneuvers caused by queueing behavior or congestion effects.

\subsection{Maneuvers}
A maneuver $M$ is defined as a temporally bounded driving or riding event performed by an ego participant $e$. Formally, a maneuver is represented by the tuple
$$M = (e,[t_\text{start},t_\text{end}], \tau, \mathcal{C},\mathbf{f}_M)$$
where  $\tau$ denotes the maneuver type, $\mathcal{C}$ optional contextual entities and $\mathbf{f}_M$ the maneuver-specific descriptors.
Depending on the maneuver type, the context can include another traffic participant (e.g. following, overtaking) and/or environmental elements (e.g. turning at an intersection).

This abstraction seperates maneuver structure (temporal bounds and context) from maneuver semantics (type-specific descriptors), ensuring consistent handling across maneuver types and facilitating downstream processing.


Most studies characterize interaction-based maneuvers (following, overtaking) using longitudinal and lateral distances defined in a relative coordinate system from the perspective of the maneuver-executing participant \cite{khan_characteristics_2001, kutsch_analyzing_2025, mohammed_characterization_2019}. This representation reduces dependency on global orientation and isolates the relative motion between interacting cyclists.

For two interacting traffic participants $e$ (ego) and $o$ (other), let $\theta_e(t)$ be the heading angle of $e$. Then the longitudinal and lateral distances are computed as:
$$\Delta x(t) = x_o(t) - x_e(t), \quad \Delta y(t) = y_o(t) - y_e(t)$$
\begin{align*}
	d_{\text{long}}(t) &= \Delta x(t)\cos\theta_e(t) + \Delta y(t)\sin\theta_e(t) \\
	d_{\text{lat}}(t)  &= -\Delta x(t)\sin\theta_e(t) + \Delta y(t)\cos\theta_e(t)
\end{align*}

Smoothing the longitudinal and lateral signals with a Gaussian kernel ($\sigma$ = 0.6s) has been found to reduce zero-crossings caused by noise and improve the identification of maneuver start and end points.


\subsubsection{Following Interactions}

Following interactions describe periods in which an ego participant maintains a consistent leader-follower relationship with another participant. Formally, a following interaction $M_F$ is a maneuver $M$ with context $\mathcal{C}=\{o\}$, where $o$ is the leader.

Candidate intervals are identified by analyzing the longitudinal displacement in the ego-centric frame for sign flips, which indicate changes in relative ordering \cite{mohammed_characterization_2019}. Additional constraints ensure meaningful following behavior, including:

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Maximum Time Headway:} threshold seperating constrained from unconstrained riding, typically around 5-6 s \cite{hoogendoorn_bicycle_2016,kutsch_analyzing_2025, bachir_identifying_2025}
	\item \textbf{Spatial constraints:} $|d_{\text{lat}}(t)|\leq 1$m and 1m $\leq d_{\text{long}}(t) \leq$ 30m
	\item \textbf{Directional consistency:} relative heading difference $\leq$ 35° \cite{feng_automatic_2023, kutsch_analyzing_2025}
	\item \textbf{Persistence:} criteria must hold for 1s 
	
\end{itemize}

The approach ensures robust detection of following behaviors by combining spatial, temporal, and directional constraints.

The descriptive feature vector $\mathbf{f}_{M_F}$ is extracted for each maneuver, and the full feature specification is provided in Table \ref{tab:following-features} in the Appendix.

\subsubsection{Overtaking Maneuver}

An overtaking maneuver describes a maneuver in which an ego participant $e$ moves from a trailing to a leading position relative to another participant $o$. Formally, an overtaking maneuver $M_O$ is a maneuver $M$ with context $\mathcal{C}=\{o, t_c\}$. The overtaking point $t_c$ denotes the timestamp at which the relative longitudinal ordering changes.

\paragraph{Detection Principle}
Overtaking is identified through the ego-centric longitudinal displacement $d_{\text{long}}(t)$. A candidate event is detected when a persistent sign change from positive to negative occurs, indicating a transition from trailing to leading position. To reduce false detections caused by noise or oscillations around zero, sign flips must persist over multiple consecutive frames. Each candidate window is then refined to determine maneuver start and end points. 

\paragraph{Automated Edge Detection}
Unlike the rule-based procedure proposed by \citeauthor{kutsch_analyzing_2025} (\citeyear{kutsch_analyzing_2025}), which was designed for precise analytical boundary identification and relied on fixed lateral distance and velocity thresholds combined with manual verification, the present work requires a fully automated and noise-robust detection strategy suitable for integration into a multi-stage clustering framework.

While the approach of \citeauthor{kutsch_analyzing_2025} enables highly accurate edge definitions for detailed scenario analysis, it is sensitive to manual threshold calibration and not directly scalable to large datasets or modular unsupervised pipelines. The objective here is therefore not maximal geometric precision but robust and reproducible maneuver segmentation.

Given a detected overtaking point $t_c$, maneuver start and end frames are determined via an adaptive threshold search on the absolute lateral displacement signal $|d_\text{lat}(t)|$.

The procedure follows three principles:

\begin{enumerate}
	\item \textbf{Local search window} A bounded backward-forward search (minimum and maximum frame limits) ensures temporal locality around $t_c$
	\item \textbf{Logarithmic adaptive thresholds} Instead of using fixed lateral distance cutoffs, a set of logarithmically spaced thresholds is generated between a minimal displacement (e.g. 0.05m) and the observed lateral distance at crossing point.
	This allows the algorithm to adapt to maneuver magnitude while remaining robust to noise and small oscillations.
	\item \textbf{Sanity validation checks} Candidate edges are accepted only if:
	\begin{itemize}
		\item longitudinal displacement evolves monotonically through the maneuver
		\item minimum lateral distance at overtaking point
		\item maximum overall lateral displacement
		\item duration exceeds a minimum threshold
	\end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}

Operationally, the algorithm searches backward from $t_c$ for the last frame, whose lateral displacement falls below the smallest feasible adaptive threshold and analogously forward for the end frame. If validation criteria are not satisfied, the candidate is discarded.

For reproducibility, the following parameters were used: a backward-forward search window of 10-125 frames, a minimum lateral separation at crossing of 0.8m, a maximum lateral displacement of 3m and a set of five logarithmically spaced lateral thresholds ranging from 0.05m to 1.2m.

The design prioritizes automation compatibility, scale robustness and reproducibility, making it suitable for hierarchical clustering applications.

For validated and complete overtaking maneuvers, the descriptive feature vector $\mathbf{f}_{M_O}$ is extracted and the full feature specification is provided in Table \ref{tab:overtaking-features} in the Appendix.

\section{Hierarchical Riding Style Identification}


This section describes the hierarchical multi-stage pipeline, which is applied to both maneuvers individually.


\subsection{General Clustering Pipeline}
\label{meth:clustering-pipeline}
A generalized unsupervised clustering pipeline was applied, designed to be domain-independent and modular to allow replacement of individual components without affecting the overall structure. Standard methods were employed to ensure interpretability, reproducibility, and computational robustness.

\begin{figure}[h!]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/clustering_pipeline.pdf}
	\caption{Flowchart of the general clustering pipeline.}
	\label{fig:clustering_pipeline}
\end{figure}


Features were filtered to reduce multicollinearity and redundancy. Initially, features with high pairwise absolute correlation were removed, retaining the feature with lower average correlation to remaining features. Subsequently, features exceeding a Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) threshold were discarded iteratively until all features satisfied the predefined criteria. The resulting matrix was standardized prior to dimensionality reduction.

Dimensionality reduction was performed via Principal Component Analysis (PCA) with whitening, retaining components until a specified explained variance threshold was achieved. Clustering was conducted in the PCA space using K-means with multiple random initializations to reduce local minima effects.

Clustering stability was quantified using the Adjusted Rand Index (ARI) under repeated 80\% subsampling, independent random seeds, and additive Gaussian noise applied to each feature, scaled by 0.4 of its standard deviation. Each measure was repeated across 100 runs, and the median, mean, standard deviation, and quantiles were reported. Geometric separation was evaluated using Silhouette, Davies–Bouldin, and Calinski–Harabasz scores, and cluster balance was monitored via the minimum cluster fraction. Stability was treated as the primary criterion, with geometric optimality considered secondary.

A grid search was conducted over correlation thresholds (0.7–1.0), VIF thresholds (3–1000), PCA explained variance (60–95\%), and the number of clusters (2–8). The final configuration was selected based on stability, cluster balance, and interpretability of cluster centroids, which were mapped back to the original feature space for domain-level interpretation.



\subsection{Window-Based Behavioral Representation}

The first stage of the hierarchical pipeline consists of segmenting each maneuver into short-term temporal windows and extracting descriptive features that characterize local riding behavior. This approach operationalizes the concept of micro-behavioral states, which capture short term variations in rider dynamics while preserving temporal continuity \cite{li_volatility-based_2025, bachir_identifying_2025, mohammadnazar_classifying_2021}.

Each maneuver is segmented using a sliding window of 1-second duration with 50\% overlap. 
The resulting windows provide a consistent temporal resolution for extracting local behavioral states, which serve as the input for regime discovery and subsequent aggregation.

\subsubsection{Feature Sets}
\label{meth:window-feature-set}

Each window is described by three distinct feature sets: riding dynamics, traffic context and infrastructure characteristics. These sets are treated independently to preserve domain seperation, ensuring that subsequent clustering reflects meaningful behavioral variation rather than the environment.

Each behavioral state is characterized by internal and external (environment) factors that make up a local behavioral state. The environment is defined by traffic and infrastructure related features.
Each feature set is standalone and serves a different purpose.

\paragraph{Riding Dynamics}

Riding dynamics capture the internal state of the ego cyclist and are derived from kinematic signals such as speed, longitudinal and lateral acceleration and yaw rate \cite{arvin_how_2019, arvin_safety_2021, bachir_identifying_2025}. 
Volatility features quantify short-term variability and fluctuations within each window. 
For each signal, the sample standard deviation, mean absolute deviation, coefficient of variation and quartile coefficient of variation are computed (Table~\ref{tab:volatility}). 
These metrics provide a principled basis for clustering windows into local behavioral regimes, reflecting micro-behavioral variation rather than absolute magnitude differences. Additionally, magnitude metrics are applied to each kinematic signal, which quantify motion intensity and include minimum, maximum and mean.

\paragraph{Traffic Context}
\label{meth:traffic}
The surrounding traffic environment is characterized using surrogate safety measures (SSMs), which quantify the operational risk posed by other traffic participants \cite{wang_review_2021}. For each timestamp within a window, proximity counts, minimal Time-To-Collision (TTC), minimal Distance-At-Closest-Approach (DCA) and Deceleration-To-Avoid-Crash (DRAC) are computed relative to all participants.
These measures are aggregated across the window to produce statistics such as mean, maximum, percentile thresholds and exposure fractions. SSMs are computed seperately for different participant classes (cyclists, pedestrians, motorized vehicles), enabling later analysis of cluster-specific interactions with distinct traffic types. The complete Traffic Feature set is provided in Table \ref{tab:traffic-features} in the Appendix.


\paragraph{Infrastructure Characteristics}
Infrastructure features describe the surrounding road environment, derived from the OpenDrive file associated with the TUMDOT-MUC dataset. Each window is characterized by metrics such as lane width, lane width entropy, lane type, lane type entropy \cite{bachir_identifying_2025}, relative lane position, offset from lane center, distance to the nearest traffic light and proportion of time spent on different surface types (bike lane, sidewalk, roadway). Empirical studies indicate that infrastructure characteristics substantially influence rider behavior and maneuver execution \cite{belikhov_exploring_2025, wang_investigating_2022, gossling_subjectively_2022}. The complete Infrastructure Feature set is provided in Table \ref{tab:infrastrucure-features} in the Appendix.



\subsubsection{Scenario Separation}
Riding dynamics vary considerably between intersections and straight road segments due to differences in environmental complexity, traffic density and rider decision-making. Empirical studies support this distinction: \citeauthor{li_volatility-based_2025} (\citeyear{li_volatility-based_2025}) observed that the volatility was substantially higher in intersection scenarios. Similarly, \citeauthor{mohammadnazar_classifying_2021} (\citeyear{mohammadnazar_classifying_2021}) found that driving styles differ across roadway contexts, with environmental factors such as street type and surrounding traffic contributing to variations in driver behavior and volatility.  

Motivated by these findings, windows are categorized into \textbf{intersection} ($I$) and \textbf{non-intersection} ($N$) groups based on the distance to the nearest traffic light (< 20~m), which provides a reliable approximation for intersection proximity. Windows in each category are clustered separately to capture context-specific behavioral regimes. 
%Subsequent maneuver-level aggregation, however, considers all windows jointly, ensuring that the final maneuver representation reflects the full spectrum of local behaviors while preserving the influence of scenario-specific complexity.


\subsubsection{Window Representation}
Let a maneuver $m$ be segmented into $T_m$ overlapping windows indexed by $t = 1, \ldots,T_m$.
Each window is defined as the tuple
$$w_t = \left(t_\text{start}^{(t)}, t_\text{end}^{(t)}, s_t, \mathbf{x}_t\right)$$
where $t_\text{start}^{(t)}$ and $t_\text{end}^{(t)}$ denote the temporal bounds of the window, $s_t\in S = \{I,N\}$ the scenario identifier (intersection or non-intersection) and 
$$\mathbf{x}_t = \left(x_t^\text{riding}, x_t^\text{traffic}, x_t^\text{infrastructure}\right)$$
the concatenated feature representation derived from riding, dynamics, traffic context and infrastructure characteristics.
The set of all windows belonging to maneuver $m$ is denoted as
$$\mathcal{W}_m=\{w_1, \ldots,w_{T_m}\}.$$
This representation forms the input space for local regime discovery described in the subsequent section.


\subsection{Stage 1: Behavioral State Discovery}
\label{meth:level1}

Building on sliding-window segmentation (Section \ref{rel:regimes}), local behavioral regimes are discovered via clustering performed separately for each scenario. Let $\mathcal{W}_m = \{w_1, \dots, w_{T_m}\}$ denote the set of windows for maneuver $m$, each with scenario label $s_t \in S = \{I, N\}$ and feature vector $\mathbf{x}_t$.  

For each scenario $s \in S$, a set of \textbf{scenario-specific regimes} $\{r^{(s)}_1, \dots, r^{(s)}_{k_s}\}$ is identified by applying the clustering pipeline to $\mathbf{x}_t^\text{riding}$ restricted to windows in that scenario. Each window $t$ assigned to scenario $s_t$ is then associated with a \textbf{window-level regime label} referencing its scenario cluster:
\[
r_{s_t,t} \in \{ r^{(s_t)}_1, \dots, r^{(s_t)}_{k_{s_t}} \}.
\]  

A globally unique, scenario-qualified regime is defined for each window as
\[
\tilde r_t = (s_t, r_{s_t,t}),
\]
which forms a consistent segmentation of riding behavior. The number of clusters $k_s$ is not fixed a priori and is selected based on stability and interpretability, allowing the framework to remain unsupervised.
All subsequent aggregation and maneuver-level representations (Section \ref{meth:aggregation}) are based on the sequence $\{\tilde r_t\}_{t=1}^{T_m}$, ensuring direct compatibility.


\subsection{Regime Aggregation}
\label{meth:aggregation}

Sequences of scenario-qualified behavioral regimes $\{\tilde r_t\}_{t=1}^{T_m}$ encode rich structural information. Rather than relying on generative models like Hidden Markov or Markov Chains \cite{pentland_modeling_1999}, these sequences are directly summarize using proportions, run lengths, temporal statistics, and entropy measures \cite{vegetabile_estimating_2019}, capturing behavioral heterogeneity without imposing stochastic assumptions.

\paragraph{Fundamental Definitions.}  
For maneuver $m$, let $T_m$ be the total number of windows, each with scenario label $s_t \in S = \{I, N\}$ and scenario-qualified regime $\tilde r_t = (s_t, r_{s_t,t})$ as defined in Section \ref{meth:level1}.  
The global regime space is
\[
\mathcal{R} = \bigcup_{s \in S} \{ (s, r^{(s)}_1), \dots, (s, r^{(s)}_{k_s}) \},
\]
ensuring unique regime identifiers across scenarios.

\paragraph{Proportions and Scenario Exposure.}  
Let $T_m^{(s)} = \sum_{t=1}^{T_m} \mathbf{1}(s_t = s)$ be the number of windows in scenario $s$.  
The scenario exposure, global regime proportions, and scenario-conditional regime proportions can be defined together as
\[
\begin{aligned}
	P_m(s) &= \frac{T_m^{(s)}}{T_m}, &\quad & s \in S, \\
	P_m(r) &= \frac{1}{T_m} \sum_{t=1}^{T_m} \mathbf{1}(\tilde r_t = r), &\quad & r \in \mathcal{R}, \\
	P_m(r \mid s) &= \frac{1}{T_m^{(s)}} \sum_{t=1}^{T_m} \mathbf{1}(\tilde r_t = r \wedge s_t = s), &\quad & r \in \mathcal{R}, s \in S.
\end{aligned}
\]
The sets of observed regimes are
\[
\mathcal{R}_m = \{ r \in \mathcal{R} \mid P_m(r) > 0 \}, \quad
\mathcal{R}_m^{(s)} = \{ r \in \mathcal{R} \mid P_m(r \mid s) > 0 \}.
\]
\paragraph{Temporal Persistence (Run Statistics).}  
A \emph{run} is a maximal contiguous sequence of windows assigned to the same scenario-qualified regime $\tilde r_t$.  
Let $L_{r,i}$ be the length of the $i$-th run of regime $r$ in maneuver $m$. The mean and standard deviation of run lengths for $r \in \mathcal{R}_m$ are
\[
\mu_r = \mathrm{mean}(L_{r,i}), \qquad
\sigma_r = \mathrm{std}(L_{r,i}).
\]

\paragraph{Temporal Center of Mass.}  
Let $T_m^{(s,r)}$ and $T_m^{(r)}$ denote the number of windows in scenario $s$ with regime $r$ and the total number of windows with regime $r$, respectively.  
The normalized center of mass is then
\[
\begin{aligned}
	\text{COM}_r^{(s)} &= \frac{1}{T_m^{(s,r)}} \sum_{t \,:\, \tilde r_t = r \wedge s_t = s} \frac{t}{T_m^{(s)}}, &\quad & r \in \mathcal{R}, s \in S, \\
	\text{COM}_r^{(m)} &= \frac{1}{T_m^{(r)}} \sum_{t \,:\, \tilde r_t = r} \frac{t}{T_m}, &\quad & r \in \mathcal{R}.
\end{aligned}
\]




\paragraph{Entropy Measures.}  
Normalized Shannon entropy quantifies behavioral variability across regimes. Define
\[
\begin{aligned}
	H_\text{global}(m) &= -\frac{1}{\log_2 |\mathcal{R}_m|} \sum_{r \in \mathcal{R}_m} P_m(r) \log_2 P_m(r), &\quad &|\mathcal{R}_m|>1, \\
	H_s(m) &= -\frac{1}{\log_2 |\mathcal{R}_m^{(s)}|} \sum_{r \in \mathcal{R}_m^{(s)}} P_m(r \mid s) \log_2 P_m(r \mid s), &\quad &|\mathcal{R}_m^{(s)}|>1,\, s \in S.
\end{aligned}
\]
If $|\mathcal{R}_m|=1$ or $|\mathcal{R}_m^{(s)}|=1$, the corresponding entropy is defined as $0$.

\paragraph{Maneuver-Level Feature Vector.}  
The aggregated maneuver-level representation is
\[
\mathbf{z}_m^\text{agg} =
\Big(
H_\text{global}(m),\,
\{ H_s(m), P_m(s) \}_{s \in S},\,
\{ P_m(r), \mu_r, \sigma_r, \text{COM}_r^{(m)}, \text{COM}_r^{(s)} \}_{r \in \mathcal{R}}
\Big).
\]
This vector captures scenario exposure, regime composition, temporal persistence, and behavioral heterogeneity, providing the input for subsequent maneuver-level analysis and clustering.


\subsection{Stage 2: Maneuver Execution Style Discovery}
\label{meth:level2}

While Stage 1 identifies local behavioral regimes, maneuvers represent structured sequences of these regimes. Stage 2 therefore abstracts each maneuver $m$ into a fixed-length representation and clusters maneuvers into \emph{execution styles}.

Given the scenario-qualified regime sequence $\{\tilde r_t\}_{t=1}^{T_m}$ for maneuver $m$, the aggregation procedure from Section \ref{meth:aggregation} produces the maneuver-level feature vector $\mathbf{z}_m^\text{agg}$.
To incorporate maneuver-type-specific characteristics (e.g., geometric or interaction descriptors), this vector is concatenated with maneuver-specific features $\mathbf{f}_M$:
\[
\mathbf{z}_m = \big(\mathbf{z}_m^\text{agg}, \mathbf{f}_M \big).
\]

Maneuvers of the same type are then clustered using the general clustering pipeline (Section \ref{meth:clustering-pipeline}) applied directly to $\{\mathbf{z}_m\}$:
\[
\mathbf{z}_m \;\;\xrightarrow{\text{clustering}}\;\; c_m \in \{1, \dots, k\},
\]
where $c_m$ is the assigned \emph{maneuver-level style label} and $k$ is selected based on stability and interpretability.

This clustering step identifies distinct maneuver execution styles, capturing structured execution patterns characterized by internal behavioral regimes.



\subsection{Stage 3: Environment-Conditioned Analysis}
Stage 3 examines how maneuver-level execution styles relate to environmental context, including both infrastructure and traffic conditions. 

For each maneuver, the environmental descriptors are summarized and compared descriptively across the maneuver execution styles to characterize the typical contextual exposure.´
Comparisons are visualized using empirical cumulative distribution functions (ECDFs), which illustrate the full distributional differences across clusters. The analysis focuses on identifying systematic patterns or tendencies, such as clusters occurring more frequently under high traffic density or in constrained intersection settings.

This stage is exploratory and descriptive; no causal claims are made. Observed differences are interpreted as contextual characterization of maneuver execution styles rather than behavioral determinants.
