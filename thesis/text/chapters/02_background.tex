Understanding cyclist behavior in urban traffic requires examining both micro-level dynamics, such as speed, acceleration, and lateral movement, and the interactions that emerge between riders during maneuvers like following and overtaking. Prior studies have employed a variety of observational, experimental, and trajectory-based methods to capture these dynamics, but often focus on isolated kinematic measures or short-term interactions without explicitly linking them to broader patterns of maneuver execution. The following sections review literature on behavioral state segmentation, multi-stage clustering, and unsupervised evaluation, highlighting approaches for capturing latent structure in cycling behavior and identifying recurring interaction patterns.

\section{Behavioral State Segmentation and Regime Discovery}
\label{rel:regimes}
% motivation for states
Cycling behavior, like driving, unfolds continuously but can be meaningfully described through short-term, discrete behavioral units \cite{pentland_modeling_1999}. Understanding these micro-level states is essential for analyzing how they aggregate into maneuver-level patterns. Prior research on behavioral state segmentation, volatility, and trajectory clustering provides a conceptual and methodological foundation for such hierarchical analysis.

\subsection*{Local Behavioral Regimes}

Trajectory segmentation aims to partition continuous motion into meaningful micro-level behavioral units. In driving research, event-based approaches have leveraged acceleration and braking patterns to identify local states \cite{yarlagadda_exploring_2022}, while sliding-window techniques capture short-term dynamics over fixed temporal intervals \cite{bachir_identifying_2025, li_volatility-based_2025, zhang_proactive_2023}. Dynamic, context-aware windows further adapt segmentation to road alignment or maneuver content, improving relevance for subsequent analysis \cite{li_unsupervised_2022}.

In cycling research, trajectory segmentation has been applied to free-flow riding conditions \cite{bachir_identifying_2025}, capturing variations in speed, acceleration, and rotational behavior. Despite the dominance of driver-focused studies in the literature, these approaches demonstrate the generalizability of micro-level state identification to cyclists. In this study, sliding-window volatility measures are extracted from riding dynamics to define local riding regimes, ensuring that clusters reflect meaningful behavioral variation while remaining independent of absolute magnitudes.

%\subsection*{Characterization of Micro-Level Dynamics}

Microscopic motion dynamics are typically characterized using kinematic measures directly derivable from trajectories. Longitudinal speed and acceleration indicate forward control, while lateral acceleration and yaw capture maneuvering and directional changes \cite{arvin_how_2019, arvin_safety_2021, bachir_identifying_2025, li_volatility-based_2025, wang_what_2015, yarlagadda_exploring_2022}. These variables form the basis for identifying behavioral regimes that summarize local motion patterns.

% volatility
Volatility has been widely applied in driving research to assess behavioral risk, with studies showing significant associations between volatility and crash frequency \cite{kamrani_extracting_2018, arvin_how_2019, zhang_proactive_2023}. Beyond risk assessment, volatility also serves as a discriminative feature for classifying distinct behavioral regimes and styles under diverse conditions \cite{mohammadnazar_classifying_2021, li_volatility-based_2025, yarlagadda_exploring_2022, wang_what_2015}. In the context of cycling, clustering on volatility captures micro-level riding patterns that are indirectly informed by risk-relevant behavior, enabling identification of regimes that are both descriptive and safety-relevant.

Scenario segmentation is critical for accurate volatility-based analysis. Driving and riding dynamics vary considerably across contexts, such as intersections versus straight segments, and these variations influence the magnitude and frequency of volatility measures \cite{mohammadnazar_classifying_2021, li_volatility-based_2025}. Careful temporal or event-based windowing ensures that local behavioral regimes reflect intrinsic riding dynamics rather than contextual artifacts.

\subsection*{Influence of Traffic and Infrastructure}

Behavioral regimes are shaped not only by individual control but also by the surrounding environment. Surrogate safety measures, such as Time-To-Collision (TTC), Deceleration-Rate-To-Avoid-Crash (DRAC), and Distance-At-Closest-Approach (DCA), quantify potential conflicts with nearby road users and provide additional context for evaluating maneuver safety \cite{wang_review_2021}. Environmental factors including topography, curvature, lane width, intersections, and wind conditions further influence riding behavior. For cyclists, studies show substantial effects of infrastructure on speed, power output, and overtaking decisions \cite{belikhov_exploring_2025, wang_investigating_2022}, while perceived safety is linked to features such as separation from motorized traffic \cite{gossling_subjectively_2022, von_stulpnagel_how_2022}.

Although traffic and infrastructure strongly influence behavior, local behavioral regimes are identified independently of these factors. Contextual variables are analyzed post hoc to interpret observed patterns, providing descriptive insight into how micro-level dynamics manifest under varying environmental conditions without constraining the unsupervised abstraction of riding behavior.

\subsection*{Aggregation of Local States}

Although probabilistic frameworks such as Markov Chains and Hidden Markov Models have been widely applied to model sequences of behavioral states \cite{khattak_analysis_2017, yin_extracting_2022, he_multi-scenario_2025} and personal driving styles \cite{meng_human_2006,zou_multivariate_2022}, their primary conceptual contribution lies in recognizing that sequences of discrete regimes encode meaningful structure. Transition probabilities between states, the proportion of time spent in each state, and entropy-based measures provide principled descriptors of behavioral complexity, capturing variability and consistency in maneuver execution without requiring explicit generative assumptions \cite{vegetabile_estimating_2019}. In this framework, a maneuver can be understood as an ordered composition of behavioral regimes, whose statistical properties summarize internal dynamics, decision patterns, and temporal variability. Analyzing environmental context post hoc further allows interpretation of how these behaviors manifest under varying traffic and infrastructure conditions, adding descriptive depth without constraining the abstraction process itself.

Approaches that identify latent behavioral states form a conceptual bridge from descriptive kinematic analysis to unsupervised style characterization. For example, prior work identifies phases of overtaking maneuvers by clustering trajectories into initiation, merging, and post-overtaking states \cite{mohammed_characterization_2019}. While this probabilistic framework captures the temporal structure of maneuver execution, it remains confined to phase-level classification without aggregating these states into higher-level behavior patterns.
Extending this idea, \citeauthor{bachir_identifying_2025} (\citeyear{bachir_identifying_2025}) proposed a two-level unsupervised clustering framework to characterize riding styles under free-flow conditions. At the first level, local behavioral regimes were identified using time-window-based riding volatility measures. At the second level, trajectory-level style representations were aggregated using features such as road type entropy, cluster percentage entropy, cluster transitions entropy, average traffic count, and average rotation fluctuation. These descriptors summarize the internal structure and variability of riding behavior, enabling unsupervised identification of latent riding styles. Although this approach demonstrates the potential of multi-level aggregation, it is not maneuver-specific and does not explicitly frame the process as hierarchical abstraction of interactions within complex traffic scenarios, motivating the methodology developed in this thesis.

Taken together, this body of work motivates a modular, hierarchical approach to cyclist behavior analysis. Local behavioral regimes capture micro-level kinematic dynamics, their composition and distribution convey structural information about maneuvers, and aggregated descriptors summarize recurring execution styles. This abstraction provides both a methodological and conceptual foundation for maneuver-level analysis, emphasizing interpretability, modularity, and stability. Building on these principles, the following section introduces multi-stage clustering frameworks as a natural extension for structuring and extracting latent patterns from aggregated behavioral regimes, linking micro-level motion to high-level riding styles in complex urban traffic.

\section{Clustering for Maneuver- and Trajectory-Level Abstraction}

Multi-stage clustering provides a systematic framework for uncovering latent patterns in complex, high-dimensional, or sequential data. Instead of applying a single clustering algorithm to raw trajectories, multi-stage approaches first identify local or intermediate patterns, aggregate them into higher-level representations, and then perform subsequent clustering to reveal global structure. This staged strategy improves interpretability, allows modular optimization, and can flexibly accommodate heterogeneous datasets \cite{nielsen_hierarchical_2016, scheiner_multi-stage_2019}.

Applications of multi-stage clustering span time series analysis, sensor data, and behavioral modeling. Typical workflows segment data into short windows, cluster each segment to capture fine-grained patterns, and then aggregate the results to derive global clusters \cite{ren_sliding_2017, nakashima_performance_2016, lai_novel_2010, guijo-rubio_time-series_2021}. Across domains, the principle is consistent: local clustering captures micro-level variation, while higher-stage clustering abstracts these into coherent, interpretable structures.

In the context of cyclist trajectories, this paradigm enables hierarchical representation of riding behavior. First, short temporal windows are clustered to identify local riding regimes that reflect variations in speed, acceleration, and directional control. These micro-level regimes are then aggregated into maneuver-level descriptors, summarizing the internal dynamics and temporal structure of each maneuver. A second-stage clustering of these maneuver descriptors reveals latent execution styles, linking fine-grained riding dynamics to recurring maneuver patterns. This hierarchical approach preserves interpretability, supports modular analysis, and provides a principled pathway from micro-level kinematics to maneuver-level behavioral abstraction.

\section{Model Validation and Stability in Unsupervised Analysis}

As a result, structural validity is typically assessed using a combination of internal criteria—such as cohesion, separation, and cluster compactness—and external agreement measures that evaluate cluster stability across multiple runs, initializations, or data perturbations \cite{liu_stability_2022, hassan_--z_2024, levine_resampling_2001}. Stability-based validation has become a central strategy for assessing clustering reliability. Early work by \citeauthor{levine_resampling_2001} (\citeyear{levine_resampling_2001}) introduced resampling methods to quantify reproducibility, showing that robust clusters are more likely to represent true underlying structure. More recent reviews emphasize that stability complements internal metrics by capturing sensitivity to data variability, initialization, and feature noise, providing a practical criterion for hierarchical or multi-stage frameworks where interpretability is essential \cite{liu_stability_2022, hassan_--z_2024}.


Although prior studies have successfully identified local behavioral states or clustered full trajectories, few approaches explicitly combine these steps to derive higher-level maneuver-style representations. By integrating micro-level dynamics into modular, interpretable abstractions and validating them through stability assessment, hierarchical clustering can uncover execution patterns relevant for both safety evaluation and traffic behavior modeling.

This literature foundation directly motivates the hierarchical, stability-oriented framework presented in the following chapter, which systematically aggregates local behavioral regimes into maneuver-level styles while ensuring robust, reproducible, and interpretable clustering outcomes.
