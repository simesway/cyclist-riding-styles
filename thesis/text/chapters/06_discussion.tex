\chapter{Discussion}


\section{Methodological Considerations}


\subsubsection{General Clustering Pipeline}
The generalized clustering pipeline reliably captured both local and maneuver-level cycling regimes, demonstrating that a simple, modular, unsupervised approach can extract meaningful behavioral patterns at each stage. K-means clustering in PCA space provided stable, interpretable partitions, with robustness confirmed under subsampling, initialization, and noise perturbations. Its modular design allows stage-specific adjustments—such as feature selection, dimensionality reduction, or clustering parameters—without affecting other stages, enabling tailored handling of local windows versus maneuver-level aggregations. These results indicate that even a basic, standard pipeline can serve as a robust baseline for hierarchical behavioral analysis.

\subsubsection{sliding windows}
Behavioral regimes were identified using 1 s sliding windows with 50\% overlap for both following and overtaking maneuvers, providing consistent feature extraction for clustering short-term riding dynamics. While this fixed-length approach proved effective, prior work suggests that window length and segmentation strategy can influence results: 1-second windows capture rapid fluctuations, longer windows reflect sustained behaviors, and intermediate lengths around 3 s balance these considerations \cite{li_volatility-based_2025}. Overlap percentage is also adjustable, and dynamic, event-driven windows -- e.g., segmentation based on acceleration or deceleration phases \cite{yarlagadda_exploring_2022} -- could further refine the detection of transient versus persistent micro-behaviors, allowing the analysis to adapt to intrinsic behavioral changes rather than arbitrary time intervals.



\section{Behavioral Findings}

\subsubsection{Maneuver Comparison with Previous Study}

Following maneuvers exhibited slightly larger longitudinal gaps and time headways compared to \citeauthor{kutsch_analyzing_2025} (\citeyear{kutsch_analyzing_2025}) (mean gap 11.16 m vs. 9.58 m; mean THW 2.67 s vs. 1.96 s), possibly due to differences in detection thresholds or filtering criteria. For overtaking maneuvers, 573 events were detected here versus 319 previously reported. Despite methodological differences—Kutsch et al. prioritized precise boundary detection while the present approach emphasizes scalability and reproducibility—maneuver durations were similar (mean 7.6 s vs. ~8 s; swerve-out 3.7 s vs. 3.8 s; merge-back 3.8 s vs. 4.17 s), indicating that the current method produces comparable results and is suitable for unsupervised analysis pipelines.

\subsection{Local Behavioral Regimes}

The clustering of overtaking maneuvers revealed two primary behavioral regimes, stable and volatile, consistent with the characterization reported by \citeauthor{bachir_identifying_2025} in free-flow conditions, who observed a similar proportion of volatile windows (~4\%). Scenario differences were pronounced: windows at intersections displayed greater volatility than those on non-intersection segments, likely reflecting increased environmental complexity. Notably, rotation fluctuation contributed strongly to the distinction between regimes at intersections. Unlike \citeauthor{bachir_identifying_2025}, who adjusted this metric to remove curvature effects, the present analysis used the raw signal, which may amplify apparent variability at intersections; accounting for curvature could refine regime separation in future work. 

Following maneuvers exhibit gradual, transient behavioral regimes rather than sharply distinct states. While clustering produced highly stable partitions across resampling and initialization, geometric separation between clusters was moderate (silhouette ≈ 0.24; Davies–Bouldin ≈ 1.40), indicating that the identified regimes reflect structured variations in longitudinal control rather than clearly bounded modes. This pattern is consistent with the continuous nature of following behavior, where cyclists dynamically adjust speed and spacing in response to a leading rider rather than switching between discrete behavioral states.

Scenario-specific differences were evident: intersection windows tended to show slightly higher variability and retained additional regimes compared to non-intersection windows, suggesting that environmental complexity modulates the degree of micro-behavioral adjustment. Overall, the moderate separation combined with high stability indicates that the identified clusters capture reproducible patterns of variation in following dynamics, without implying rigid, categorical modes.


\subsection{Maneuver-Level Execution Styles}

\subsubsection{maneuver-level aggregation}
The aggregation of local behavioral regimes into maneuver-level representations highlights pronounced structural differences between overtaking and following maneuvers. Following maneuvers typically consist of multiple, shorter regime segments, reflecting ongoing adjustments and continuous modulation of riding dynamics during following interactions. In contrast, overtaking maneuvers are often dominated by a single regime that persists throughout most of the maneuver, producing a cohesive, event-like pattern. These differences persist even when comparing maneuvers of similar duration, indicating that the observed structural patterns are not simply a consequence of maneuver length but instead reflect distinct temporal organization.

Scenario exposure further reinforces this distinction: overtaking is largely concentrated at intersections, consistent with localized, context-dependent execution, whereas following can span multiple segments and exhibits more internal variability. Temporal metrics, including run lengths and centers of mass, indicate that regimes can appear at varying points within maneuvers, particularly in following, highlighting the fragmented and dynamically adaptive nature of these behaviors. Together, these findings underscore a conceptual distinction: overtaking maneuvers are event-driven and temporally cohesive, whereas following maneuvers are progressive, continuous, and responsive to scenarios, providing a structural basis for subsequent clustering of maneuver types in Stage 2.

\subsubsection{overtaking maneuver-level execution styles}
The five identified overtaking maneuver clusters reveal how traffic context and rider strategy shape execution styles. Intersection-related overtakes are captured in slow and cautious overtakes (Cluster 0) and accelerating overtakes (Cluster 4). Cluster 0 reflects careful adjustments in constrained intersection environments, with moderate speeds and a balanced mix of stable and volatile regimes. Cluster 4 represents accelerating overtakes, likely occurring immediately after traffic lights or stop-go situations, highlighting bursts of effort triggered by external cues.

In mixed or free-flow conditions, sustained high-speed overtakes (Cluster 1) and smooth high-speed overtakes (Cluster 3) dominate. Cluster 3 corresponds to typical smooth free-flow maneuvers, with stable speeds and minimal variability, representing the “prototypical” passing behavior, while Cluster 1 reflects longer, high-speed overtakes with continuous, controlled acceleration.

Finally, high-steering overtakes (Cluster 2) capture short-duration maneuvers with pronounced lateral motion. Given its low frequency and extreme lateral values, this cluster likely represents rare, constrained situations or residual noise from the overtaking detection algorithm.

Taken together, these clusters illustrate how environmental context (intersection vs. free-flow) and rider strategy (acceleration bursts, steady progression, lateral adjustments) interact to produce distinct overtaking styles. Importantly, this hierarchical, multi-stage unsupervised approach achieves good separation of maneuver-level execution styles. The regime aggregation step plays a crucial role, as cluster differentiation is primarily driven by scenario exposure and dominant micro-regimes, effectively bridging local behavioral states to interpretable maneuver-level patterns.

\subsubsection{following maneuver execution styles}
At the maneuver level, clusters highlight meaningful differences in interaction intensity and temporal organization, but they should be interpreted as gradations of behavior rather than fully discrete types. Short or quick maneuvers, typically occurring at intersections, show higher lateral variability, reflecting reactive adjustments to environmental complexity. Longer or spaced maneuvers, concentrated in non-intersection segments, exhibit steadier speed and spacing, indicating more consistent tracking.

Clusters labeled “catch-up” or “quick adjustment” capture higher-intensity interactions with stronger acceleration and shorter headways, whereas “spaced cruising” or “prolonged close following” reflect lower-intensity, more stable tracking. These distinctions demonstrate that execution styles are structured and reproducible, yet remain gradual, mirroring the underlying micro-regime variability observed in Stage 1.

These clusters reveal reproducible patterns of follower behavior that transition gradually, reflecting moderate separation between regimes rather than sharply distinct modes.


\subsection{Structural and Contextual Insights}

The conditioning analysis in Stage 3 was designed to characterize the contextual exposure of maneuver execution styles identified in Stage 2. Because execution styles were discovered without environmental variables, any observed differences in traffic or infrastructure metrics reflect descriptive associations rather than causal influences. This framing emphasizes that Stage 3 examines \textit{where} certain execution styles tend to occur, not \textit{why} they emerge.

Within both intersection- and non-intersection–dominant clusters, infrastructure variables such as lane width and lane type showed minimal variation. This confirms that differences in execution styles are not driven by geometric features within the same scenario class, allowing interpretation to focus on traffic exposure metrics.

Overtaking maneuvers exhibited moderate associations with environmental conditions. Accelerating intersection overtakes were generally accompanied by higher pedestrian conflict indicators (DRAC and TTC), whereas slow intersection overtakes occurred under lower exposure. Non-intersection overtakes were largely similar across clusters, with minor differences in bicycle counts. The small sharp-steering overtakes cluster (n = 16) produced stepwise ECDF curves, limiting its interpretability. These observations suggest that overtaking behavior may partially adapt to surrounding traffic demands, though the effects are associative rather than causal.

In contrast, following maneuvers showed limited differentiation across clusters once scenario context was controlled. ECDF distributions largely overlapped, and the ordering across clusters was inconsistent across metrics and participant types. This indicates that following styles are primarily driven by leader–follower interaction dynamics, including gap regulation, speed matching, and response timing, rather than by broader environmental conditions. This finding aligns with Stage 2 observations, where following clusters were more gradual and less distinct than overtaking clusters, highlighting the intrinsic nature of interaction-driven behavior.

The scenario-based separation applied in Stage 3 effectively controlled for environmental context, such as intersection versus non-intersection segments. This separation clarifies where maneuvers tend to occur, suggesting that most environmental variation is captured by the scenario split. In contrast, the intrinsic execution characteristics of each maneuver — including speed profiles, acceleration patterns, lateral movements, and regime dynamics — describe how maneuvers are performed. Within scenario-dominant clusters, these execution styles showed minimal differentiation across traffic exposure metrics, indicating that the internal structure of maneuvers appears largely independent of the immediate environment. In other words, the scenario split accounts for the location and context of maneuvers, while execution style within those contexts seems to reflect intrinsic behavioral strategies, a pattern that is particularly evident for following maneuvers.

The limited differentiation in Stage 3 may partly reflect the metrics used. TTC and DRAC, while standard in traffic safety, were designed for motorized vehicles and may not fully capture cyclist–cyclist or cyclist–pedestrian interactions. Metrics tailored to active mobility could provide greater explanatory power.

Another methodological aspect concerns the temporal aggregation of environmental indicators. In the present analysis, traffic exposure metrics were aggregated across entire maneuvers to enable cluster-level comparisons. Aggregating exposure metrics over the full maneuver duration may smooth localized interaction effects, potentially reducing observable differences between clusters. More targeted analyses that focus on specific maneuver phases or regime sequences could therefore reveal more detailed relationships between behavior and environment.

Furthermore, cyclists may actively select when to initiate maneuvers based on perceived environmental opportunities. Overtaking, for instance, is likely initiated only when sufficient lateral space or acceptable interaction conditions are present. Such behavioral self-selection effectively filters environmental conditions before the maneuver begins. Consequently, the environmental context observed during a maneuver may already represent a subset of situations that permit that behavior, which can reduce variability in the conditioning analysis.

The conditioning analysis nevertheless demonstrates an important methodological property of the proposed framework. Maneuver execution styles were identified independently of environmental descriptors, relying solely on trajectory-derived behavioral patterns. The resulting clusters therefore capture intrinsic execution structures rather than context-dependent artifacts. This separation enables the systematic study of maneuver styles across different environments and locations. In the context of drone-based trajectory datasets, this capability allows the pipeline to be applied to a wide range of infrastructure settings, supporting comparative analyses of cyclist behavior under varying traffic and geometric conditions.


\section{Limitations}

Some clusters, particularly those with few maneuvers (e.g., high-steering overtakes), have limited sample sizes, which constrains interpretability and statistical generalization. Larger datasets or multi-site recordings could provide more robust estimates of rare execution styles.

\section{Future Directions}

Cyclist behavior during complex maneuvers such as overtaking and following is still poorly understood, particularly regarding execution styles and interactions with environmental context. The hierarchical framework developed here captures both micro-level riding dynamics and maneuver-level execution patterns while remaining agnostic to infrastructure, providing a systematic basis for cross-scenario comparisons. Because the underlying data is drone-based, trajectories can be efficiently collected and analyzed across diverse locations and infrastructure types, enabling scalable studies of cyclist behavior in realistic settings. This approach offers a foundation for modeling, predicting, or simulating complex cyclist interactions, linking raw trajectory data to interpretable behavioral insights.

The hierarchical framework was designed to be agnostic to the choice of input features. In this study, sliding-window riding dynamics, maneuver-level descriptors, and descriptive infrastructure and traffic metrics were employed, but the methodology does not depend on any particular feature set. Alternative representations—such as different riding features, micro-regime definitions, or additional context descriptors—can be incorporated without modifying the pipeline. The framework therefore provides a general approach for the discovery and aggregation of local and maneuver-level behavioral patterns. While no systematic evaluation was performed to determine which features produce the strongest clusters, the modularity of the pipeline allows for future refinement and fine-tuning of feature sets to suit different datasets or research objectives.
