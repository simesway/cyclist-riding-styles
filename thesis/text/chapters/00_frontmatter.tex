\chapter*{Abstract}

Understanding cyclist behavior during complex maneuvers is essential for improving urban traffic safety, infrastructure design, and behavioral modeling. This thesis proposes a hierarchical, unsupervised framework to identify cyclist riding styles from trajectory data, focusing on overtaking and following interactions. The framework consists of three stages: first, maneuvers are decomposed into short temporal windows to capture local kinematic dynamics; second, these micro-regimes are aggregated into maneuver-level representations that summarize temporal persistence, regime proportions, and variability; third, execution styles are analyzed in relation to environmental context such as traffic exposure and infrastructure characteristics.
The analysis reveals clear differences between overtaking and following maneuvers. Overtaking behavior is characterized by two primary micro-regimes—stable and volatile—and typically forms event-driven sequences with persistent motion once initiated. Five distinct overtaking execution styles emerge, differing in speed profiles, acceleration strategies, lateral dynamics, and contextual conditions. In contrast, following maneuvers exhibit gradual longitudinal adjustments and continuous control dynamics. Six following execution styles capture variations in gap size, responsiveness, duration, and speed adaptation, highlighting frequent short-term adjustments rather than discrete behavioral states.
Environmental context shows only limited influence on execution style differentiation. While minor variations appear in overtaking maneuvers at intersections with higher interaction exposure, following styles are largely shaped by cyclist interaction dynamics rather than surrounding infrastructure or traffic conditions.
Methodologically, the study introduces a modular multi-stage framework that transforms raw trajectory interactions into interpretable behavioral representations at both micro and maneuver levels. Empirically, it identifies distinct execution styles and reveals fundamental differences in the temporal organization of overtaking and following maneuvers. These findings contribute to improved cyclist behavior modeling and provide insights relevant for traffic simulation, safety assessment, and urban mobility planning.
